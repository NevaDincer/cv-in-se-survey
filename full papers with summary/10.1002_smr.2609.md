# Image-based communication on social coding platforms

> **SCOPE WARNING**: This paper does not apply any computer-vision or image-processing technique. It is a mining and survey study about the prevalence and perceived value of images in developer communication on Bugzilla and Stack Overflow. Every step that touches image content is done by humans: three hired developers manually open-sort a sample of 2,000 images into categories, 165 more developers manually classify 22,561 images into those categories, and 168 surveyed developers manually judge whether each image is informative and whether the accompanying text is understandable without it. No automated image analysis, feature extraction, or model of any kind is run on the images themselves; the only quantitative analysis performed is descriptive statistics and nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis) on metadata like post length, reply counts, and time-to-close. The paper's own related-work section and conclusion explicitly frame CV techniques (object detection, image captioning, CNNs) as unexploited future work, not as something this study does. It belongs in a CV-in-SE survey only as motivating evidence for why such techniques are needed, not as an example of a CV technique applied to an SE task.

- **Year:** 2023
- **Venue/Source:** Journal of Software: Evolution and Process, Special Issue - Empirical Paper
- **DOI:** 10.1002/smr.2609

## Metadata

- **se_area:** maintenance (issue tracking and bug triage on Bugzilla) blended with general developer communication and knowledge sharing (Q/A on Stack Overflow)
- **se_task:** characterizing how often, in what form, and to what perceived benefit developers attach images to bug reports and Q/A posts, and whether image presence correlates with faster or more engaged community response
- **cv_technique:** none applied. All image content analysis (categorization into ten content types, informativeness judgments) is performed manually by human annotators through crowdsourced surveys; the paper only discusses CNN-based object detection, image captioning, and image generation as future directions, not as methods used in this study
- **platform:** web (Bugzilla issue tracker and Stack Overflow, both browser-based social coding platforms)
- **rationale:** not applicable; the paper does not select or justify a CV technique because it does not use one. Its argument for future CV adoption is context-driven: images are said to carry information (GUI state, error dialogs, code screenshots) that text alone omits, so an automated system would need to read that visual context to fully understand a post
- **artifact_type:** full-interface screenshots and photos as shared informally by developers (dominant categories: code/IDE screenshots, run-time error dialogs, menus and preferences, program input/output, steps-and-process illustrations, CPU/GPU performance graphs, algorithm/concept diagrams). These are naturally occurring, uncontrolled artifacts pulled from real issue reports and Q/A posts, not a curated benchmark
- **eval_method:** mixed-methods empirical study, not a technique evaluation. Quantitative: 9 years (2013-2022) of Bugzilla data across four Mozilla projects (65,611 bug reports) and Stack Overflow data tagged Python/Java/JavaScript (1,391,224 threads, 3,364,177 posts), analyzed with descriptive statistics, Mann-Whitney tests, Cliff's delta effect sizes, and Kruskal-Wallis tests across eight hypotheses. Qualitative/survey: 168 crowdsourced developers (recruited via BountySource plus Mechanical Turk, minimum two years' experience) categorized and rated 22,561 sampled images (5,187 from Bugzilla, 17,374 from Stack Overflow), with three senior developers (Cohen's Kappa = 0.83) establishing the ten content categories from an initial 2,000-image sample. Main numbers: 87.8% of images judged informative, 86.9% of associated posts judged hard to understand without the image, image-bearing Bugzilla issues closed about twice as fast (p = 0.03) and got about twice as many replies (p = 0.001) as text-only issues, Stack Overflow questions with images got faster accepted answers (33.1% faster, p = 0.01) and higher acceptance rates
- **challenges:** author-reported: the study is explicitly descriptive/exploratory and cannot establish causality between image presence and outcomes like faster closure or more replies; potential confounding factors in the Bugzilla/Stack Overflow data were not controlled for; survey responses may carry response bias toward positive answers; six of eight Kruskal-Wallis hypotheses in RQ5 showed no significant difference between image content types; findings are limited to two platforms (Bugzilla, Stack Overflow) and may not generalize to others; no automated image mining was attempted, so the paper cannot speak to feasibility or accuracy of any CV pipeline, only to the potential value of building one

## Summary

This paper is an empirical, non-CV study of how software developers use images when communicating on social coding platforms, extending an earlier ICSE 2020 NIER paper with four more years of data and additional analyses. The authors mine nine years (2013-2022) of Bugzilla bug reports from four Mozilla projects and Stack Overflow Q/A posts tagged Python, Java, or JavaScript, tracking how the proportion of posts containing images changed over time. They then have crowdsourced developers manually categorize a large sample of images into ten content types (code/IDE screenshots, run-time errors, dialog boxes, menus, program input/output, and others) and judge, post by post, whether the image adds information beyond the text and whether the text alone would be understandable. Finally they compare response metrics (reply counts, time to close, time to first or accepted answer) for posts with and without images using Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests.

The results show a steady rise in image sharing on both platforms, that 87.8% of sampled images were judged informative and 86.9% of accompanying text insufficient without the image, and that image-bearing posts on both platforms received faster and more engaged responses than text-only posts. The paper concludes that current issue trackers and Q/A tools ignore visual content entirely, and argues that software engineering automation (bug triage, code search, communication assistants) will need to incorporate image processing techniques such as object detection or image captioning to keep pace with how developers actually communicate. No such technique is built or tested here; the contribution is the empirical case for why one is needed, based entirely on manual human coding of the images rather than any automated visual analysis.
