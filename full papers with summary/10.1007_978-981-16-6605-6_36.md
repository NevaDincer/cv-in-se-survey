# Optical Character Recognition for Test Automation Using LabVIEW

- **Year:** 2022
- **Venue/Source:** Sustainable Communication Networks and Application, Lecture Notes on Data Engineering and Communications Technologies, vol. 93 (Springer)
- **DOI:** 10.1007/978-981-16-6605-6_36

## Metadata

- **se_area:** testing
- **se_task:** automated verification of embedded/infotainment software LCD display output, specifically comparing an actual on-screen test image against a reference/expected image during regression testing of automotive infotainment systems
- **cv_technique:** Optical Character Recognition (OCR), implemented in LabVIEW's vision toolkit, combined with classical image-preprocessing steps: grayscale conversion, 3x3 mean/smoothing (noise-reduction) filtering, region-of-interest (ROI) selection, and threshold-based character segmentation (auto light/dark background thresholding, spacing/size configuration)
- **platform:** general/embedded (automotive infotainment display hardware, i.e., a physical LCD screen captured via an industrial camera rather than a mobile or web app)
- **rationale:** robustness (the paper's central argument is that raw pixel-to-pixel / image-similarity comparison is unreliable across frames with different lighting/intensity, so extracting and comparing the actual displayed text is more accurate than comparing pixels directly)
- **artifact_type:** localized (a specific region of interest on the display, e.g., the "Settings" label, is cropped and OCR'd, rather than the whole screen being parsed)
- **eval_method:** a single qualitative case-study demonstration on one automotive infotainment device connected to Apple CarPlay ("Apple Care" in the text). The pipeline is shown extracting the string "Settings" from a highlighted ROI on the LCD front panel, with a reported classification/identification score per character. The paper claims, without a quantitative benchmark, statistical comparison, or numeric accuracy/precision figures, that the OCR-based method is more accurate than the traditional pixel-to-pixel comparison method it replaces.
- **challenges:** the paper's own stated motivation/limitation is that plain image-similarity/pixel-difference algorithms are not accurate enough for LCD verification because digital images captured at different times have different intensity values, making absolute pixel differencing unreliable; the OCR approach itself required font-specific training/enhancement to work on the embedded vision software's rendered fonts; no quantitative accuracy metric, ground-truth dataset, or comparison baseline numbers are reported, and the evaluation is limited to a single demonstrated example/device rather than a systematic test suite.

## Summary

This short paper targets a specific pain point in embedded and automotive software testing: automatically verifying that the correct image or text appears on an LCD or infotainment display, a task that has historically resisted automation because prior image-comparison methods, pixel-to-pixel or holistic similarity, aren't accurate enough. Different capture times yield different pixel intensities even for a visually correct screen, so those methods produce unreliable pass/fail verdicts. The authors propose replacing, or supplementing, raw image comparison with OCR. An industrial camera grabs an RGB image of the physical display under test, the image is converted to grayscale and smoothed with a 3x3 mean filter to reduce noise, a region of interest is selected and thresholded (with an auto light/dark background mode and configurable character spacing and size), and an OCR engine, trained and enhanced for the specific embedded-vision fonts inside LabVIEW's vision toolkit, extracts the actual on-screen text, such as recognizing the string "Settings," which can then be compared against the expected reference text for a pass/fail decision. Each recognized character carries a classification and identification score. The paper's literature review touches on medical-image similarity metrics, biometric face-based attendance systems, and prior LabVIEW-based test-script generation, though most of these connections feel loose. The evaluation itself is a single case study on automotive infotainment hardware connected to Apple CarPlay: no quantitative accuracy numbers or statistical testing back up the claim that OCR-based extraction beats pixel comparison. The conclusion is appropriately modest, framing the contribution as combining pixel comparison with OCR-based extraction depending on the verification need, not replacing pixel comparison outright.
