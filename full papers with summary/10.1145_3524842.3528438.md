# CLIP meets GamePhysics: Towards bug identification in gameplay videos using zero-shot transfer learning

- **Year:** 2022
- **Venue/Source:** MSR 2022 (19th International Conference on Mining Software Repositories), May 23–24, 2022, Pittsburgh, PA, USA
- **DOI:** 10.1145/3524842.3528438

## Metadata

- **se_area:** testing (game testing / bug identification and reproduction)
- **se_task:** Searching/mining large repositories of gameplay videos using free-form English text queries to identify objects and (bug-related) events, in order to support game physics bug identification and bug report reproduction for game developers.
- **cv_technique:** CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-Training) used in zero-shot transfer mode, with two backbone architectures compared (ResNet-based "RN101" and Vision Transformer-based "ViT-B/32"); frame-level image embeddings and text-query embeddings compared via cosine similarity; Faiss used for efficient large-scale similarity search; two custom frame-to-video score-aggregation methods devised by the authors (max similarity score per video; count of frames within a top-k "pool" of highest-ranked frames per video).
- **platform:** game (PC video games; gameplay videos collected from Reddit's GamePhysics subreddit)
- **rationale:** Ease of use / avoiding labeling cost. The paper explicitly motivates its choice of CLIP zero-shot transfer over supervised classification because supervised approaches "require a training dataset, a test dataset, and a fixed number of classes," which is "demanding and labour-intensive" to maintain; CLIP avoids manual data labeling and model training/fine-tuning entirely while still supporting arbitrary English queries. Context-driven robustness is also cited (flexibility to handle any English text query and generalize across diverse games without retraining).
- **artifact_type:** temporal (video-based; approach processes gameplay videos frame-by-frame across time, aggregating per-frame similarity scores over the whole video); also natural-input in the sense that queries are free-form English text rather than fixed labels/classes.
- **eval_method:** Custom-collected "GamePhysics" dataset of 26,954 gameplay videos from 1,873 games scraped from the Reddit GamePhysics subreddit (via PushShift.io + Reddit APIs, youtube-dl/aria2c for downloads, filtered for spam/low score/length 2–60s). Evaluation subset: 8 popular open-world games (6,192 videos, 23% of dataset) chosen for diverse genre/visual style. No prior benchmark existed, so authors designed 3 experiments with custom manually-authored queries (informed by manual review of 80 sampled videos): (1) Experiment 1 (22 simple object queries), measured with top-1 and top-5 binary accuracy; (2) Experiment 2 (22 compound queries) (object + descriptor), also top-1/top-5 accuracy; (3) Experiment 3 (44 game-specific bug queries), measured with recall@5. Two CLIP backbones (RN101, ViT-B/32) compared as baselines against each other (no external baseline system compared, partly because prior gameplay-video-mining work like Luo et al. requires labeled training data and is not directly comparable). Results: Exp1 average top-1/top-5 accuracy 60%/76% (ViT-B/32) and 64%/86% (RN101); Exp2 average top-1/top-5 78% (ViT-B/32) and 82% (RN101); Exp3 average recall@5 66.12% (ViT-B/32) and 66.35% (RN101). Evaluation also included qualitative/manual inspection by the first author of returned videos (including false positives) to characterize failure modes.
- **challenges:** Authors explicitly report: (1) adversarial poses: CLIP misclassifies familiar objects (e.g., a vertically-positioned car) when seen from unusual camera angles/poses, which are common due to physics bugs; (2) confusing textures/patterns causing false matches (e.g., a striped wall mistaken for a "parachute"); (3) confusion among visually similar object categories, especially different vehicle types (cars/bikes/airplanes/tanks) and different four-legged animals (dogs/cats/deer/wolves/cows/horses); (4) CLIP's emergent OCR-like ability to "read" in-scene text can cause it to match a query to a video merely because that text appears on-screen, unrelated to the actual visual content (noted as both a potential feature and a bug); (5) no benchmark dataset exists for gameplay bug videos, so a 0% score cannot be distinguished from "no matching videos exist in the dataset" versus true model failure (internal validity threat); (6) the pool-size hyperparameter (1,000) in the second aggregation method was chosen by manual trial and error and not systematically tuned, so results in Experiment 3 may be sensitive to this choice; (7) external validity concerns: the curated GamePhysics dataset (bug-heavy, videos capped at 60s, zero-score/spam posts excluded) may not generalize to non-curated video sources (e.g., streaming footage) or to longer videos; (8) pre-processing (extracting CLIP embeddings for every frame of every video) is computationally expensive, requiring dual NVIDIA Titan RTX GPUs, which the authors flag as the most costly step and discuss speed/accuracy trade-off mitigations (lower precision, model binarization, layer pruning) as future work.

## Summary

This paper proposes a zero-shot approach for mining large repositories of gameplay videos using free-form English text queries, aimed at helping game developers locate and reproduce "game physics" bugs (e.g., object clipping, ragdoll glitches, objects stuck in geometry) without any manual video labeling or model training. The method encodes every video frame and the input text query with the pre-trained CLIP model (comparing ResNet-based RN101 and ViT-B/32 backbones), computes cosine similarity between text and frame embeddings using Faiss for efficient large-scale search, and aggregates per-frame scores into a per-video relevance score via two proposed methods (maximum frame score, and count of frames within a top-ranked pool). To evaluate the approach in the absence of any existing benchmark, the authors constructed and released the GamePhysics dataset, 26,954 gameplay videos from 1,873 games scraped from Reddit's GamePhysics subreddit, and ran three experiments (simple object queries, compound queries, and 44 hand-crafted bug queries) on a curated subset of 8 open-world games, reporting top-1/top-5 accuracy and recall@5. Results show the approach can identify objects (60–86% accuracy) and bug-related events (~66% recall@5) without any fine-tuning, though the authors' own manual review of false positives surfaces notable weaknesses such as confusion between similar-looking object/animal classes, sensitivity to unusual object poses caused by the very physics bugs being sought, and CLIP's emergent tendency to match on in-scene text rather than visual content. The main contribution is establishing CLIP-based zero-shot video-text retrieval as a viable, label-free alternative to prior gameplay-video-mining methods that required manual annotation and fixed class sets.
