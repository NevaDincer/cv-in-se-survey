## Metadata

- **se_area:** not applicable / does not map cleanly to a software-engineering lifecycle stage, this is closest to "development" only in the trivial sense that the authors built a software prototype (EnviroLink), not because CV is being applied to a software-engineering task.
- **se_task:** none in the SE sense; the actual task is assistive human-computer interaction, using facial/head/eye/lip movement analysis to drive an adaptive, accessible user interface for people with quadriplegia.
- **cv_technique:** MediaPipe (pre-trained facial-landmark detection/tracking model) for identifying and tracking facial keypoints (eyes, mouth, head pose); OpenCV for video capture, contrast/brightness/noise-reduction filtering, feature detection, and optical-flow-based motion tracking of facial points.
- **platform:** desktop (Windows PC with a USB camera; the "interface" being adapted is the assistive control software itself, not a mobile/web app under test)
- **rationale:** ease of use / accessibility (the entire motivation is making digital interaction possible for users with severe motor impairment) and context-driven adaptation (interfaces personalized to each user's specific range of facial/head motion).
- **artifact_type:** full-interface in a loose sense (the system tracks the whole face/head to drive an entire custom control interface), but this does not map to the survey's usual GUI-artifact categories since there is no software UI being reverse-engineered, tested, or generated. The "interface" produced is a bespoke accessibility control panel, not a mockup/screenshot/app screen.
- **eval_method:** small, informal user study, 3 anonymous subjects simulating different quadriplegia conditions, each performing scripted head-turn/mouth-open/blink actions across two ~1-2 minute recorded sessions in a controlled lab room; results reported as 6 successful vs. 2 failed test videos plus qualitative heat-map/movement-graph reports and per-subject qualitative observations (e.g., glasses interfering with blink detection). No precision/recall/F1 or comparison against a labeled ground truth, and no baseline comparison against other systems (only a narrative literature comparison).
- **challenges:** authors report visual noise from cluttered backgrounds overloading MediaPipe and slowing the system (mitigated with a white background); inconsistent lighting causing missed eye/mouth events (mitigated with controlled lighting); high CPU/RAM load from the CV pipeline (mitigated by resolution reduction); reflections off shiny surfaces causing false positives; and difficulty keeping facial-point tracking stable during fast movements (mitigated with additional smoothing filters). They also note the prototype still needs better accuracy across lighting/movement conditions and testing on a broader, more diverse participant pool (n=3 is explicitly a limitation).

## Summary

EnviroLink is a software prototype that uses computer vision to help people with quadriplegia interact with a computer, tracking head, eye, and mouth movements and mapping them into a personalized, adaptive control interface. A USB camera feeds real-time video into OpenCV, handling capture, filtering, and optical-flow-based motion tracking, and Google's MediaPipe, handling facial-landmark detection and tracking. A central "CORE" component orchestrates all of this, driven through a Pygame-based GUI. Detected facial events (blinks, mouth opening, head displacement) get plotted in real time on an X/Y movement map with color-coded event types, logged to CSV via Pandas, and used to build a per-user movement footprint meant to inform interface personalization. The related-work section places EnviroLink against other assistive-interaction systems, EMG-based gesture recognition, wheelchair control via OpenCV and Arduino, eye and voice HMI systems, arguing its edge is flexibility across input types rather than winning on any single benchmark. The evaluation is small and exploratory: three subjects simulating different degrees of quadriplegia performed a scripted set of head, mouth, and eye actions, yielding 6 successful and 2 failed capture sessions plus qualitative observations, such as glasses interfering with blink detection. The authors iteratively worked through background visual noise, lighting variation, computational load, reflective-surface interference, and tracking jitter during fast motion. Their stated future plans, LLM-driven interface generation, real-time adaptive learning, broader participant testing, and haptic feedback, lean accessibility-focused rather than software-engineering-process-focused.
