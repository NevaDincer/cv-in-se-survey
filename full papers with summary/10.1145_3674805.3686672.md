# Data Analysis Tools Affect Outcomes of Eye-Tracking Studies

> **SCOPE WARNING**: questionable fit.** Like the companion ETRA '23 paper in this batch, this is not an application of computer vision to a software-engineering artifact. It is a meta-/methodology study about the *eye-tracking data analysis tool landscape* used in prior SE eye-tracking research (systematic mapping of 97 papers) plus a re-analysis of three public datasets with two open-source gaze-analysis tools (Ogama, PyGaze). The "algorithms" involved (velocity-threshold, dispersion-threshold, minimal-spanning-tree, I2MC fixation/saccade classifiers) operate on raw gaze-coordinate time series, not on images/screenshots/GUIs, and are not typically categorized as computer vision. Recommend excluding or flagging for a methodology-adjacent category rather than the core CV-in-SE corpus.

- **Year:** 2024
- **Venue/Source:** ACM/IEEE ESEM '24 (International Symposium on Empirical Software Engineering and Measurement), Barcelona, Spain

## Metadata

- **se_area:** Cross-cutting / research methodology (spans development, code comprehension, debugging, wherever eye tracking is used in SE)
- **se_task:** None specific to an SE deliverable; the "task" studied is the reliability/reproducibility of SE *research methodology* that uses eye tracking to study developers
- **cv_technique:** None applied to SE artifacts. Fixation/saccade detection algorithms (velocity-threshold, dispersion-threshold, minimal spanning tree, I2MC) used by eye-tracking analysis tools (Tobii software, iTrace, Ogama, PyGaze) are gaze-signal classification methods, not image-based CV techniques.
- **platform:** General (applies across whatever platform the underlying SE eye-tracking studies used: code editors, screens)
- **rationale:** Not applicable in the CV-adoption sense; the paper's motivation is reproducibility and standardization of eye-tracking methodology in SE research
- **artifact_type:** Not applicable. Outputs are statistical metrics (fixation counts, durations, correlation coefficients, effect sizes) rather than any CV-derived SE artifact
- **eval_method:** (1) Systematic mapping study of 97 SE eye-tracking papers (IEEEXplore + ACM Digital Library) coding which analysis tools were used/justified; (2) three re-analysis case studies re-running published statistical analyses with Ogama and PyGaze on public datasets (EMIP dataset via Aljehane et al., Peitek et al.'s EEG+eye-tracking dataset, Sharafi et al.'s data-structure task dataset), comparing correlation coefficients, Mann-Whitney tests, and effect sizes to the originally published results
- **challenges:** ~33% of surveyed papers did not report which analysis tool was used at all; only ~32% justified their tool choice, and consequences were rarely discussed; re-analysis showed different tools (even with default settings) can flip statistical significance and even qualitative conclusions on the same raw data; two of three re-analyzed studies lacked sufficient parameter documentation to fully replicate; the I2MC algorithm is non-deterministic, further hurting reproducibility.

## Summary

This paper is a two-part empirical study on the *tooling* used to analyze eye-tracking data in software-engineering research, not a study that applies computer vision to an SE artifact. Part I is a systematic mapping study of 97 SE papers using eye tracking (extending Sharafi et al.'s 2015 SLR), finding that roughly a third of papers never name their analysis tool, and that when justifications are given they mostly cite functional features (e.g., scrolling support, mapping gaze to code elements) rather than validity concerns. Part II re-analyzes three publicly available eye-tracking datasets from prior SE studies (on code-reading expertise, programmer efficacy, and data-structure problem solving) using two open-source tools, Ogama and PyGaze, with default settings, and compares the recomputed correlations, group-difference tests, and effect sizes to the originally published numbers. The central finding is that the choice of analysis tool alone, independent of the raw gaze data, can change numeric results, statistical significance, and even the qualitative conclusions researchers draw, sometimes contradicting the original paper's claims. The authors recommend extended reporting guidelines (tool name, algorithm, and parameters) to improve reproducibility in the SE eye-tracking literature. The paper's relevance to a CV-in-SE survey is limited: it concerns human-eye-movement signal processing and empirical-methodology validity, not visual/GUI artifact analysis by machine.
