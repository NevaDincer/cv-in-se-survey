# How Should I Improve the UI of My App?: A Study of User Reviews of Popular Apps in the Google Play

- **Year:** 2021
- **Venue/Source:** ACM Transactions on Software Engineering and Methodology (TOSEM), Vol. 30, No. 3, Article 37
- **DOI:** 10.1145/3447808

## Metadata

- **se_area:** Maintenance / requirements evolution (post-release feedback loop informing UI changes); also touches requirements elicitation indirectly (feature requests inferred from reviews)
- **se_task:** Empirically characterizing UI-related complaints in app store reviews and studying developer-user dialogue patterns around UI issues
- **cv_technique:** None. The approach is purely textual: n-gram/word2vec-based keyword extraction to identify UI-related reviews, followed by manual open coding of review text (and review-response dialogue text) to build a taxonomy. No screenshots, images, or rendered UI are analyzed.
- **platform:** Mobile (Google Play Store apps, free and non-free, spanning 59 categories/sub-categories including Games)
- **rationale:** Not applicable (no CV technique employed)
- **artifact_type:** Not applicable (no visual artifact analyzed; the artifact under study is review text)
- **eval_method:** Large-scale quantitative mining (3.3M+ UI-related reviews from 22,199 free and 9,380 non-free apps), statistically-sized manual sampling and open coding (samples sized at 95-99% confidence level/5% interval) with inter-rater agreement via Fleiss Kappa (0.76 for issue taxonomy, 0.79 for dialogue patterns), Wilcoxon signed-rank significance tests on rating changes, an anonymous validation survey of 43 app owners/developers, and triangulation against a sample of GitHub issues from 1,046 open-source apps on F-Droid
- **challenges:** Reviews are often "generic" (16% of labeled reviews give no actionable detail, e.g. "the UI is ugly"), which limits what any automated or manual technique can extract; app stores don't support image attachments in reviews, so visual UI problems must be described in words only, causing information loss; existing automated review-tagging approaches are reported as not accurate enough for this fine-grained a category, hence the reliance on labor-intensive manual coding (~40 reviews/hour per coder); Google Play only exposes the 500 most recent reviews per app, constraining historical sampling.

## Scope note

This paper likely falls outside the survey's scope. The study relies entirely on text mining and manual open coding of user reviews (app reviews); no images, screenshots, or visual data are processed at all. In other words, none of the techniques used in the paper are computer vision (there's keyword extraction, word2vec similarity, Wilcoxon test, Fleiss Kappa, all NLP/statistical methods, but no CV). Although "accessibility" and "UI issue" categories do come up, these are extracted from user-written text, not from visual analysis. ## Summary

This paper is a large-scale empirical study, not a tool paper, and it never touches computer vision. It is included here only because it is squarely about UI quality in mobile software engineering. The authors mine over 3 million UI-related reviews from 31,578 top Google Play apps (22,199 free, 9,380 non-free) using keyword/n-gram/word2vec-based filtering, then manually open-code a statistically representative sample of 1,447 reviews to build a taxonomy of 17 UI issue types grouped into four categories: Appearance (layout, legibility/color, typography, iconography, image), Interaction (navigation, notification, motion, gesture, accessibility), Experience (redundancy, customization, advertisement, feedback), and Others (generic, comparative, design-specification reviews). They find UI-related reviews rate apps notably lower than overall reviews (3.68 vs 4.17 mean), and that "Comparative Review" and "Advertisement" issues carry the highest share of 1-2 star ratings. A second analysis looks at how app owners respond to UI complaints in review threads, identifying eight dialogue patterns (most common: "Apology or Appreciation," then "Information Request"), and shows that active developer engagement, especially timely UI updates and specific advice, measurably raises subsequent ratings. The study is validated through a developer survey and cross-checked against GitHub issue trackers for a subset of open-source apps, finding the taxonomy is judged useful by developers as both a diagnostic checklist and a team communication guideline. Given the absence of any visual/CV component, its main potential relevance to a CV-in-SE survey would be as background motivation (why UI-in-general matters) rather than as a CV-technique data point.
