# Comparison of Claude (Sonnet and Opus) and ChatGPT (GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-o1) in Analyzing Educational Image-based Questions from Block-Based Programming Assessments

- **Year:** 2025
- **Venue/Source:** 2025 13th International Conference on Information and Education Technology (ICIET)
- **DOI:** 10.1109/ICIET66371.2025.11046263

## Metadata

- **se_area:** Not a standard SE lifecycle phase. The closest analog is education/training support for programming (block-based/visual programming comprehension); it could be loosely mapped to "maintenance/comprehension" if forcing an SE-lifecycle label, but the paper's own framing is K-12 programming education and automated assessment/grading support.
- **se_task:** Automated answering and analysis of image-based block-based-programming exam questions (multiple-choice and fill-in-the-blank), as a proxy for potential downstream SE-education tasks: personalized tutoring, automated grading/feedback on block-based code, and generation of programming exercises
- **cv_technique:** End-to-end multimodal image understanding built into six commercial LLMs, GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, GPT-o1 (OpenAI), and Claude-3 Opus, Claude-3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic). No separate or explicit CV algorithm (OCR, CNN, object detection) is applied by the authors; images of exam questions were uploaded through each chatbot's web interface with an identical prompt, and the model's internal, proprietary, unspecified vision-language mechanism was treated as a black box.
- **platform:** General (desktop/web chat interfaces; the content analyzed is block-based programming scripts, i.e. visual/graphical programming, but this is unrelated to mobile/web/desktop software platforms in the usual SE sense)
- **rationale:** Ease of use and accessibility, using off-the-shelf chatbot image upload rather than building a custom vision pipeline. Also context-driven, since the models must interpret programming semantics embedded in a graphical block layout.
- **artifact_type:** Full-interface / whole-image. Each exam question, including the full block-based script and multiple-choice options, was captured as a single image and given to the model in its entirety. No localized cropping, temporal/video, or natural free-form input was used.
- **eval_method:** 20 images (15 multiple-choice and 5 fill-in-the-blank questions) from the December 2019 Level-1 National Youth Programming Ability Level Test (NCT), presented identically to all six chatbots through Google Chrome on December 6, 2024. Accuracy was computed per model per question type and overall, and a Pearson correlation test (via SPSS/Python) was run across model response-pattern pairs to assess whether models share interpretive mechanisms. The dataset and results were released on GitHub.
- **challenges:** GPT-4 and GPT-4o mini showed uniformly low accuracy (0.20 across all categories), suggesting limited image-based reasoning capacity in earlier or lighter models. Claude-3 Opus answered zero of five fill-in-the-blank questions correctly, indicating particular difficulty generating free-form textual answers, as opposed to selecting among predefined multiple-choice options, from image-based programming questions. The study is limited to a single 20-question test administered once per model with no repeated trials or robustness check, and it relies on each vendor's proprietary, versioned, non-reproducible black-box vision pipeline, a limitation the authors themselves note given how frequently these models are updated.

## Scope note

Questionable fit for the CV-in-SE survey, for two reasons. First, the "CV technique" here is not a discrete, inspectable computer-vision algorithm (SIFT, CNN feature extraction, OCR); it is the opaque built-in multimodal image-understanding capability of six general-purpose commercial LLM chatbots (GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, GPT-o1, Claude-3 Opus, Claude-3.5 Sonnet), used end-to-end through a chat UI with no visibility into or control over the underlying vision pipeline. Second, the software-engineering-lifecycle relevance is weak. The task is K-12/primary programming education and assessment, answering block-based programming exam questions from images, not a software engineering task from the requirements, design, development, testing, or maintenance lifecycle in the sense the survey targets. It sits closer to CS-education and programming-pedagogy research that happens to involve reading block-based-program screenshots. Recommend flagging for the author's judgment on whether "reading/understanding block-based program screenshots" counts as an SE-adjacent artifact-comprehension task, or whether this falls outside the survey's SE scope entirely.

## Summary

This study evaluates six contemporary multimodal chatbots, GPT-4, GPT-4o, GPT-4o mini, GPT-o1, Claude-3 Opus, and Claude-3.5 Sonnet, on their ability to solve image-based questions from a standardized elementary block-based-programming assessment, the National Youth Programming Ability Level Test. The authors frame it as the first systematic evaluation of OpenAI's then-new GPT-o1 image-analysis capability in this domain. Twenty exam-question images (15 multiple-choice, 5 fill-in-the-blank), captured in Simplified Chinese and covering block-based constructs such as if-else conditionals, were uploaded identically to each chatbot through its web interface, and responses were scored against the standard answer key. GPT-o1 achieved the highest overall accuracy at 80 percent (87 percent multiple-choice, 60 percent fill-in-the-blank), which the authors attribute to more advanced image-analysis mechanisms, followed by GPT-4o at 60 percent and Claude-3.5 Sonnet at 55 percent. GPT-4 and GPT-4o mini lagged badly at 20 percent each, and Claude-3 Opus, despite reasonable multiple-choice performance, failed every fill-in-the-blank item. A Pearson correlation analysis found a significant positive correlation (r = 0.612, p < 0.01) between GPT-4o and GPT-o1 response patterns, which the authors read as evidence of shared architectural lineage or training data, while other model pairs, including the two Claude models against each other and against the GPT family, showed weak correlations. This motivates a discussion of potentially combining complementary models. The authors discuss downstream applications such as personalized tutoring, AI-driven exercise generation, and automated grading, but they do not implement or evaluate any of these applications directly. The core contribution is the comparative accuracy and correlation benchmark itself.
