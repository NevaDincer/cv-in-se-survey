# Data-Driven Accessibility Repair Revisited: On the Effectiveness of Generating Labels for Icons in Android Apps

- **Year:** 2021
- **Venue/Source:** ESEC/FSE 2021 (29th ACM Joint European Software Engineering Conference and Symposium on the Foundations of Software Engineering), Athens, Greece
- **DOI:** 10.1145/3468264.3468604

## Metadata

- **se_area:** Maintenance (accessibility repair for existing apps)
- **se_task:** Empirically re-examining and improving automatic content-description ("alt-text") label generation for unlabeled icons in Android apps to support screen-reader accessibility; specifically revisiting and critiquing the prior LabelDroid approach and proposing coala, a context-aware label generation model
- **cv_technique:** Transfer-learned CNN image encoder (fine-tuned ResNet18, pretrained then adapted on Rico-derived icon-class annotations) for icon image embedding, fused via a fully-connected layer with a separate Context Encoder (one-hot encoding for categorical fields like app category/screen region, GloVe word embeddings + LSTM for textual usage-context fields like Android id, screen title, sibling text) into a joint representation decoded token-by-token by an LSTM-based Label Decoder (sequence generation with teacher forcing); benchmarked against the image-only LabelDroid (CNN image-captioning) baseline
- **platform:** Mobile (Android)
- **rationale:** Robustness (the paper's central empirical argument is that image data alone is insufficient/unreliable for label prediction due to severe class imbalance and identical icon images requiring different labels depending on usage context (e.g., a "plus" icon meaning "create new contact" vs. "add a playlist") and context-driven (explicitly incorporates icon usage-context information, app/activity/icon-level metadata, as the mechanism for disambiguating visually similar but functionally distinct icons)
- **artifact_type:** Localized (per-icon image crops combined with structured, non-visual usage-context metadata, i.e. a hybrid localized + non-visual/textual artifact)
- **eval_method:** Large-scale empirical/data-exploration study of 21,864 icons from 9,658 apps addressing three RQs on label distribution/uniqueness, visual-similarity-to-label diversity, and correlation/mutual-information between contextual sources and label tokens; critical re-evaluation of LabelDroid revealing severe accuracy inflation from predefined (Android-SDK-shipped) icon labels (accuracy on predefined labels 0.90 exact match vs. only 0.17 on non-predefined labels) and demonstrating that class-balancing/downsampling does not fix this; head-to-head automatic evaluation of coala vs. LabelDroid on non-predefined labels using Exact-match, BLEU-1/2/3, METEOR, ROUGE-L, CIDEr (coala outperforming across all metrics, e.g., 0.38 vs. 0.14 exact match); a human user study (156 icons, 514 filtered crowd responses via Google Forms, 1-5 Likert quality ratings) comparing ground truth, coala, LabelDroid, and random labels, with Wilcoxon signed-rank hypothesis testing confirming coala's statistically significant superiority over LabelDroid (though both still fall short of ground-truth developer labels); training/inference performance measurement (241 min average training, 17ms average per-icon inference)
- **challenges:** Central author-reported finding/challenge: automatically-collected icon-label datasets are highly imbalanced/long-tailed (93.5% of 3,061 distinct labels have fewer than 5 samples; a handful of Android-SDK predefined labels dominate), which silently inflates reported accuracy of prior data-driven approaches like LabelDroid and invalidates naive evaluation; image content alone is demonstrably insufficient to disambiguate visually similar icons with different intended functions across apps; qualitative failure analysis of coala itself shows errors from uninformative/missing contextual metadata, confusing sibling-element text, insufficient training examples for infrequent icon classes, and cases where model outputs are semantically valid alternatives (or the ground truth itself is wrong) yet are penalized by automatic NLP metrics, motivating the added human evaluation.

## Summary

This paper critically re-examines LabelDroid, the widely cited deep-learning approach for automatically generating accessibility labels for unlabeled icons in Android apps, and proposes an improved context-aware alternative called coala. Through a large empirical study of 21,864 icons extracted from 9,658 apps, the authors uncover a severe, previously unexamined problem: the automatically-collected label dataset is highly imbalanced, dominated by a small set of "predefined" labels that ship with standard Android Studio widgets (e.g., "navigate up," "search"). They show that LabelDroid's reported accuracy is almost entirely driven by its strong performance on these easy, already-labeled-in-practice predefined icons (0.90 exact match) versus its much weaker performance on genuinely unlabeled, non-predefined icons (0.17 exact match), the actual target use case, and that simply rebalancing the training data does not fix this. Building on further empirical findings that identical icon images can require entirely different labels depending on usage context, the authors develop coala, which fuses a fine-tuned ResNet18 image encoder with a Context Encoder that embeds structured usage-context signals (app category, screen region, Android element ID, sibling elements' IDs/text) via one-hot encoding and GloVe+LSTM, feeding an LSTM-based sequential label decoder. In head-to-head evaluation on the harder non-predefined label subset, coala substantially outperforms LabelDroid across all standard image-captioning metrics (e.g., 0.38 vs. 0.14 exact match), a result corroborated by a human user study with statistically significant preference for coala's labels over LabelDroid's (though both still trail actual developer-written ground truth). A notable detail is the paper's broader methodological contribution as a cautionary case study: it demonstrates how naive benchmark evaluation of data-driven SE/accessibility tools can produce misleadingly optimistic results when the underlying real-world data distribution is not carefully audited for bias.
