# Neat: Mobile App Layout Similarity Comparison Based on Graph Convolutional Networks

- **Year:** 2024
- **Venue/Source:** Companion Proceedings of the 32nd ACM International Conference on the Foundations of Software Engineering (FSE Companion '24)
- **DOI:** 10.1145/3663529.3663832

## Metadata

- **se_area:** testing
- **se_task:** automated mobile-app GUI layout regression testing: non-intrusive comparison of two screenshots (same page rendered on different devices/resolutions or across app versions) to detect layout anomalies such as misalignment, missing elements, misplaced elements, and squeezing, plus localization of the specific mismatched GUI components
- **cv_technique:** YOLOX (and comparisons against Faster-RCNN, SSD, YOLOv5) for GUI component object detection; OCR for text recognition; Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) applied to a graph built from detected components (nodes = components with YOLOX-derived features, edges = spatial/positional associations) to produce a whole-page layout embedding via triplet loss; a separate Siamese network (SimCLR backbone, contrastive loss) for fine-grained GUI-component-level similarity matching/localization; Euclidean distance and IoU (Intersection over Union) as similarity metrics; SSIM used only as a comparison baseline
- **platform:** mobile (Android and iOS)
- **rationale:** robustness (device/resolution/OS fragmentation means screenshots of the "same" page can differ greatly in size and aspect ratio, and classical pixel-based methods like SSIM require identical resolutions) and ease of use (the tool is explicitly "non-intrusive": no source-code instrumentation or platform-specific accessibility-tree/XML extraction is needed, so one method works uniformly across Android and iOS without device-and-OS-specific scripts)
- **artifact_type:** full-interface (whole-page screenshots are decomposed into a page-level layout graph/descriptor for similarity comparison) combined with localized (once two pages are flagged dissimilar, individual GUI components are matched/localized via the Siamese network to pinpoint missing or misplaced elements); long screenshots are additionally handled via a sliding-window crop into smaller sub-screenshots
- **eval_method:** three-RQ empirical evaluation: RQ1: GUI component detection accuracy (mAP, FPS) across Faster-RCNN/SSD/YOLOv5/YOLOX on a 10,000-page dataset (50% RICO, 50% industrial screenshots; YOLOX selected for best accuracy/speed trade-off), and layout-similarity precision/recall vs. IoU threshold plus a head-to-head comparison against SSIM on 5,000 industrial page pairs across five page-type categories (Neat: 0.895 average precision vs. SSIM: 0.649); RQ2: GUI-component-matching precision/recall using a Siamese/SimCLR model trained on 9,000 of 10,000 sampled component images, with distance-threshold tuning via precision/recall curves; RQ3: six-month industrial deployment across multiple ByteDance app teams reporting real-world precision (~90%) by page type, call volume (83,000 calls/month), issues found (120/month), and estimated labor savings (13.8 person-days/month).
- **challenges:** SSIM-based baseline comparison requires resizing images to identical resolution/aspect ratio, which the paper argues is unreliable when aspect ratios genuinely differ across devices; detection and downstream similarity accuracy is markedly worse on irregularly-designed "publicity" pages versus uniformly-structured "commodity" pages, since GUI component detection itself is less accurate on non-standard layouts; the industrial deployment shows an approximate 10% false-positive rate (dissimilar layouts judged similar), which the product team currently deems acceptable but which the authors note requires continued bad-case collection and refinement; a simpler earlier layout-descriptor approach (10×10 grid flattened to a 100-d vector) worked on simple layouts but produced many false positives on complex, component-dense pages, motivating the switch to the GCN-based descriptor; the paper does not report false-negative/missed-issue costs from the 10% error rate in the production deployment.

## Summary

This paper presents Neat, a "non-intrusive end-to-end mobile App layout similarity measurement Tool" built at ByteDance to address the high manual cost of verifying that a given app page renders correctly and consistently across the many device models, screen resolutions, and OS versions found in real-world Android/iOS fragmentation. Rather than relying on platform-specific accessibility/XML tree extraction (which requires device instrumentation and differs across OSes) or classical pixel-similarity metrics like SSIM (which require identical image resolutions), Neat treats layout comparison purely as a computer-vision problem over screenshots. It first runs a trained YOLOX object-detection model (chosen over Faster-RCNN, SSD, and YOLOv5 for its accuracy/speed trade-off) plus OCR to locate and classify GUI components (text, icon, image, pop-up, keyboard, button) on each page; it then encodes the detected components and their spatial relationships as a graph and applies a Graph Convolutional Network, trained with triplet loss, to produce a single layout-similarity descriptor per page, compared via Euclidean distance and IoU thresholds. When two pages are judged dissimilar, a second, independently trained Siamese network (a SimCLR backbone with contrastive loss) matches individual GUI components between the pages to localize exactly which elements are missing or misplaced, giving developers actionable, marked-up diagnostic output rather than a bare pass/fail signal. Evaluated on RICO and 10,000 industrial screenshots, Neat's layout-similarity approach achieves 0.895 average precision versus 0.649 for SSIM across five page-type categories. Perhaps the most notable aspect of the paper is its "lessons learned" framing: after a six-month production deployment across multiple ByteDance app teams (83,000 monthly invocations, 120 real layout issues surfaced per month, ~13.8 engineer-days/month saved), the paper candidly reports a roughly 10% false-positive rate and explicitly frames the tool's precision as merely "acceptable" to the product line rather than solved, positioning ongoing bad-case collection as future work, an unusually transparent account of a CV-based SE tool's real production limitations rather than a purely favorable lab result.
