# Automated evaluation of game content display using deep learning

- **Year:** 2024
- **Venue/Source:** EASE 2024 (28th International Conference on Evaluation and Assessment in Software Engineering)
- **DOI:** 10.1145/3661167.3661184

## Metadata

- **se_area:** Testing (game/graphics quality assurance testing)
- **se_task:** Automated testing of in-game camera content correctness: detecting camera clipping artifacts, incorrect/unintended objects in a camera's view, and incorrect camera position/orientation (transform) relative to the 3D scene, in a commercial soccer video game
- **cv_technique:** Vision Transformer (ViT-Huge, pretrained, fine-tuned via transfer learning) for binary clipping detection; VideoMAE (masked autoencoder video model, adapted from action-recognition to classify camera view content as correct/incorrect) for viewing-content evaluation; MiDaS (monocular depth estimation) to convert camera output into relative depth space; Siamese network (fine-tuned, using ViT-derived visual embeddings of depth images) for similarity-based evaluation of camera transform (position/orientation) correctness
- **platform:** Game (3D game engine, evaluated in a commercial EA Sports soccer game prototype; explicitly claimed as generalizable to simulation software and digital twins)
- **rationale:** Ease of use / cost reduction (manual camera QA inspection is estimated at ~20% of total game testing effort; automation reduces this to ~4%); context-driven (each camera "cluster", e.g., spectators, pitch, goal, cinematic, gets its own trained model reflecting cluster-specific correct content); robustness (adding noise to depth images during training improves model robustness/generalization)
- **artifact_type:** Full-interface (whole rendered camera-view images/video sequences) and temporal (16-frame video sequences processed via VideoMAE for viewing content evaluation)
- **eval_method:** Empirical evaluation over a full 12-month game development cycle (~2500 cameras on average); automatically/semi-automatically constructed dataset (200 videos per cluster, 12 clusters, 2400 videos total, split into good/bad); false positive/false negative counts averaged over 18 QA test sessions (104 videos per session) per pipeline stage (clipping, view, transform); reported ~87% reduction in issues detected during QA/by end users compared to prior human-only-tested years; reported 21x speedup in clipping detection via the ViT-based model vs. geometric intersection tests
- **challenges:** Only the "bad" example sets can be fully automated (via scripted object manipulation); "good" example sets still require QA/human selection, limiting full automation of dataset creation; performance/parameter choices (ViT, MiDaS, Siamese network) were selected based on state-of-the-art and experimentation but may vary by use case and require re-tuning/fine-tuning per client/project; thresholds for flagging errors were chosen empirically to balance false positives/negatives and are project-specific; the method depends on rendering system's depth buffer or a depth-estimation model, which may introduce noise

## Summary

This paper addresses a game-industry-specific quality assurance problem: as a 3D game's environment evolves during development (objects added, removed, or moved), previously configured in-game cameras (static or cinematic) can silently break, showing clipped/cut-off objects, revealing unintended content, or ending up positioned/oriented incorrectly relative to the scene, and manually re-checking thousands of cameras (the paper's case study game has ~2500) is estimated to consume ~20% of total QA effort. The authors build a three-stage automated pipeline combining several adapted deep-learning computer vision models: a fine-tuned Vision Transformer (ViT-Huge) performs binary clipping detection on rendered frames; an adapted VideoMAE (originally designed for human action recognition from video) is repurposed to classify whether a camera cluster's viewed content is correct, given 16-frame video sequences; and camera transform (position/orientation) correctness is evaluated by converting the camera output into a depth map (via either the game engine's ground-truth depth buffer or the MiDaS monocular depth estimation model) and comparing it to expected good/bad examples using a fine-tuned Siamese network operating on ViT-derived embeddings. Evaluated over a full year-long production cycle of a commercial EA Sports soccer game prototype, the system reduced estimated camera-QA effort from ~20% to ~4% of total testing effort and was associated with an ~87% reduction in camera-content issues found in QA and by end users compared to previous fully-manual years, while the ViT-based clipping detector alone achieved a 21x speedup over traditional geometric intersection tests. The paper is notable as (to the authors' knowledge) the first published work applying deep learning specifically to validate camera transform/positioning correctness in 3D game/simulation environments, and for creatively repurposing an action-recognition video model (VideoMAE) and a depth-estimation model (MiDaS) for a software-testing quality-assurance task outside their original domains.
